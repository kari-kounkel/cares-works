Why Your P&L and Your Tax Return Don't Match (and Which One the Bank Believes)

Two documents, two different profits, one very confused owner. Nobody lied. Here's what happened, and how to make them agree before someone with a checkbook notices.

Kari Hoglund Kounkel · CARES Consulting · 6-minute read

Somewhere in your files right now are two numbers that should be the same and aren't. One is the net income on the profit and loss report you pull from QuickBooks. The other is the net profit on the tax return your CPA filed. If you've never compared them, go do that. I'll wait.

If they match to the dollar, congratulations, you are in a tiny minority and you can stop reading. If they're different by a few thousand or a few hundred thousand, you're normal, and you've got a problem that doesn't hurt until the exact moment you need it not to: a loan application, a sale, an audit, a divorce, a partner buyout. Every one of those hands your two numbers to a stranger and asks them to pick.

Sidebar Note: "Let your yes be yes" applies to net income too. One number. Not a range.

The five ordinary reasons they don't match

1. The CPA adjusted, and nobody told the books

This is the big one. Your bookkeeper closes December. In March, the CPA looks at the file and makes adjusting entries: depreciation, accrued expenses, a reclass of that loan payment that got coded to an expense, the entertainment disallowance, the owner's health insurance. Those adjustments live on the CPA's workpapers and on the return. They almost never get entered back into QuickBooks. So the return reflects a version of the year that the books have never heard of. Do that three years running and the gap is a canyon.

2. Cash versus accrual

Many small businesses keep books on accrual (invoices count when sent) and file taxes on cash (income counts when collected). Both are legitimate. But a $40,000 invoice sent December 28 and paid January 10 is income this year on the books and next year on the return. Multiply by every open invoice and bill on December 31 and the two documents are describing two different calendars.

3. Depreciation only exists on one side

The CPA takes Section 179 or bonus depreciation on the truck and the equipment. That's a real deduction that lowers taxable profit. But most bookkeepers never post depreciation, so the books show the truck sitting at full cost forever and net income higher than what was taxed. The return looks poorer than the books. A lender sees the return.

4. Things that aren't deductible got deducted (in the books)

Client entertainment, which hasn't been deductible in years. Penalties and fines. The owner's life insurance. Political contributions. The gym. These are expenses in the books, so book profit is lower. The CPA adds them back on the return, so taxable profit is higher. Now the return shows more profit than the books, which is the rare case where the mismatch flatters you, and the lender still doesn't like it because it means someone had to fix your file.

5. Owner money went through the wrong door

Owner draws coded as payroll. Personal expenses coded as business. Loans from the owner coded as revenue. Loan payments coded as expenses instead of split between principal and interest. Each one is a small miscategorization; together they shift tens of thousands between the P&L and the balance sheet, and the return, prepared by someone who caught some of it, disagrees with the books, prepared by someone who caught none of it.

Which one wins

The tax return. Always. It was signed under penalty of perjury and filed with the government; your QuickBooks file was signed by nobody. When a banker or a buyer sees a mismatch, they don't average the two. They take the return, treat the books as unreliable, and then apply a discount for the unreliability. You get the lower number and a haircut.

What "tied to the return" actually means

It doesn't mean the books and return show identical net income. It means there's a one-page schedule that starts with book net income, lists every difference with a reason, and lands on the return's number. Book income $412,000, less depreciation $18,000, plus non-deductible entertainment $2,400, less accrued December invoices $8,400, equals return income $388,000. Signed, dated, filed with the year. That schedule is what a lender means when they say your books are "clean." Not perfect. Explained.

You can build that schedule for one year in an afternoon if the books are reconciled. If they aren't, you're building on sand and you'll do it twice. Reconcile first, tie second.
